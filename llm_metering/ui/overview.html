Agent platform · latency investigation

We've been watching the wrong gauge

Our monitoring tracks how many calls we send to the AI provider, and that number looks healthy. But the provider also limits how much text we send. Our best current explanation is that we're running out of the second budget while the first one still looks fine — which is exactly why the dashboard shows nothing wrong.

The same moment, two different budgets

Both bars below are measured at our busiest moment in a simulated run of our own traffic pattern. The provider enforces both limits. Our dashboard only shows the first one.

Why this hides so well

Agents run about ten steps each, and every step re-sends the entire conversation so far. So our text volume grows far faster than our call count. One call at step ten can carry twenty times the text of the same call at step one — but the dashboard counts them both as "one call".

What could be causing it

We started with five possible explanations and tested each one against what we actually observe. Four are still live; one is ruled out. We deliberately have not narrowed it further, because the remaining four need different fixes and we can't yet tell them apart.

The surprise: our retries are helping, not hurting

We went in assuming our automatic retries were making things worse. The testing says the opposite. When a call is refused, retrying is what eventually gets it through — without retries, a large share of calls simply fail instead.

But retrying is also where the long wait comes from. Each attempt waits a little longer than the last, so a call that succeeds on its seventh attempt has spent most of that time waiting between tries. The 40-second wait isn't the provider being slow. It's us queuing politely.

The trade we're actually making

Turning retries down would shorten the worst waits and turn them into outright failures instead. That's a product decision, not a technical one: is a call that takes 40 seconds better or worse than a call that doesn't happen? For most of our agents we think slow beats failed — but it should be a choice we make on purpose.

How the worst-case wait grows with each extra retry

Each row is the longest wait our worst-affected calls could reach, at a given number of attempts, while typical calls still take about a second. This is how we know roughly how many retries are really happening in production today.

What we're asking for

Three steps, in order. The first is small and unblocks everything else.

  1. Add two numbers to our monitoring

    We already collect most of what we need — the provider tells us our remaining budget on every single response, and we're saving it. We need two more fields: how much of our conversation history the provider is reusing for free, and a proper count of refused calls. That's the difference between guessing and knowing.

    Small change to the shared client · unblocks the decision below

  2. Read the answer from real traffic

    With those fields, the four remaining explanations become distinguishable within days of normal running. No experiment, no load test — just look.

    No engineering work · one analysis pass

  3. Then turn on traffic metering

    A service every call passes through that paces our requests — the same idea as the traffic lights on a motorway on-ramp. It's built and tested, and it currently runs in observe-only mode: it records what it would have done without changing anything.

    Built already · stays in observe-only until step 2 answers
Why not just turn it on now

One of the five explanations — a bottleneck inside our own service — would be made worse by pacing our traffic, not better. We ruled it out by reasoning, not by measurement. Until step 2 confirms that, switching on metering blind is a real risk of spending effort making things slower.

What this does not tell us yet

  • These numbers come from a simulation, not from production. We modelled the provider's published rules and our own traffic shape. It narrows five explanations to four and tells us what to measure. It does not tell us which one is actually true.
  • Several inputs are estimates. Where we didn't know a number, we tested a range of values rather than picking one. Findings that hold across the whole range are marked as such in the technical detail; those that don't are flagged as provisional.
  • More than one cause may be true at once. We are not expecting a single culprit.
  • We built this to be able to prove ourselves wrong. The testing harness has checks confirming it can return the inconvenient answers — including "nothing is wrong at the provider, the problem is ours" and "the proposed fix makes things worse".
